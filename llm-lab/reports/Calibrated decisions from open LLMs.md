# Turn open LLMs into calibrated deciders

You can build a Jev-style "System One" decision model from open weights today with mostly standard tools. Treat the LLM as a backbone, the way you would fine-tune a ViT. Run one prefill pass over the input and read a learned head (or the label-token logits) that gives one logit per allowed option. Train it with a proper scoring rule (cross-entropy, optionally plus Brier), then fit a temperature on held-out labels. A conformal layer on top gives coverage-guaranteed abstention. This is almost exactly what Cloudflare's open-weight Clef publicly describes: a frozen Qwen backbone, LoRA adapters, and a joint "schema head" trained with label-smoothed cross-entropy plus Brier loss. TypeSafe has said far less about its own model, Jev. It has published no paper, model card, reliability diagram, ECE or Brier score for Jev or for its "RLCD" training method, and no independent calibration test exists for either Jev or Clef. The research literature explains why the head-plus-temperature route works. Pre-trained token probabilities are well calibrated, but RLHF and binary-reward RL sharpen them toward 0 and 1, and the fixes are either a proper scoring rule in the objective or post-hoc temperature scaling. The setup that fits one A100 40GB is bf16 LoRA on Qwen3.5-4B or -9B, not QLoRA, which Unsloth advises against for Qwen3.5. For sperm motility from CASA kinematics, the honest expectation is that the LLM will *not* beat a calibrated gradient-boosted tree or TabPFN on eight numeric features. Its plausible value lies in few-shot transfer to new labs and in fusing free-text metadata.

## Pre-training gives calibrated logits and post-training removes the calibration

There are three ways to read "confidence" from an LLM. **Token probabilities** are the softmax mass on the answer tokens, the direct analogue of max-softmax in an image classifier. **Verbalized confidence** means the model writes "80%", which is just more generated text. **Sampling consistency** means drawing K answers and measuring how often they agree, or computing semantic entropy over clusters of answers with the same meaning; it is the analogue of ensemble or MC-dropout disagreement. The founding result is that large *pre-trained* models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and that the RLHF policy's miscalibration is largely fixable by rescaling the temperature ([Kadavath et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). The GPT-4 report gives the canonical picture: on an MMLU subset, **ECE went from 0.007 for the pre-trained model to 0.074 after RLHF** ([OpenAI 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)). Later work confirmed that instruction tuning and RLHF worsen calibration ([Zhu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13240)). It also traced RLHF overconfidence partly to reward models that favour confident-sounding answers whatever their quality ([Leng et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09724)).

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR), the recipe behind reasoning models, makes calibration worse when the reward is binary correctness alone. RLCR measured this precisely on HotPotQA. RLVR raised accuracy from 39.7% to 63.0% while **AUROC fell to chance (0.50)**. Adding a Brier term to the reward kept accuracy at 62.1%, cut ECE to 0.03 and lifted AUROC to 0.69 ([Damani et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806)). Out of distribution, RLVR's calibration was *worse than the base model's* (Brier/ECE 0.46), while RLCR held at about 0.21. That is better, but still far from calibrated. Reasoning helps verbalized confidence in relative terms: reasoning models were better calibrated than their non-reasoning counterparts in 33 of 36 settings ([Yoon et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14489)). In absolute terms they remain overconfident, often stating 85–100% when wrong on SimpleQA and GPQA, with ECE almost perfectly anticorrelated with accuracy (r ≈ −0.96 to −1.00) ([Mei et al., EACL Findings 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18183)).

No elicitation method wins everywhere, and 2026 work shows that the rankings themselves are fragile. In 12 settings with 7–8B open models, changing only *where* the answer was scored (a bare query versus a "Guess:" slot) flipped the ECE winner between verbalized and token confidence in 4 settings and the AUROC winner in 9. The choice of ECE estimator flipped none ([arXiv 2605.27752](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27752)). Verbalized confidences are sparse, clustering at values like 80, 90 and 95. That damages ranking metrics and makes binned ECE unstable ([ACL Findings 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1671.pdf)). For long-form answers, only semantic consistency of the final answer was calibrated across 685k responses from up to 20 LLMs ([arXiv 2602.00279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00279)). Semantic entropy remains the best-known detector of confabulation for free text ([Farquhar et al., *Nature* 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0)). The most practically important result for a decision model is that *training beats prompting*. A LoRA "calibration-tuning" run on about **1,000 graded examples** beats every prompting-only method and generalizes across tasks, with gains plateauing around 5,000 examples ([Kapoor et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08391)).

Measurement deserves as much care as method. ECE is bin-sensitive and confounded by accuracy ([arXiv 2609.26489](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.26489v1)). After controlling for accuracy, many reported calibration advantages weaken or reverse ([Yang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30814)). RLCR's own table shows why ECE alone misleads: RLVR "improved" ECE from 0.53 to 0.37 while its ability to rank right from wrong answers collapsed. The defensible reporting set is accuracy, a proper score (NLL or Brier), ECE with stated binning, AUROC or a risk–coverage curve, a reliability diagram, and the exact prompt. Multiple-choice setups add two LLM-specific traps. Models put prior mass on particular option letters, a "token bias" seen across 20 LLMs ([Zheng et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03882)). Reordering options alone shifts accuracy substantially ([Pezeshkpour & Hruschka 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11483)). None of nine major model families reports calibration metrics in its releases ([arXiv 2609.26489](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.26489v1)), so you must measure calibration yourself.

## A learned head plus temperature scaling makes up most of a System One model

A decision model wants one forward pass that returns a calibrated distribution over a fixed set of options. Four method families deliver pieces of that, and they stack.

**Zero-shot label-logit scoring** is the no-training baseline. Append the options and an answer cue, run one prefill pass, restrict the last-position logits to the K label tokens, and apply a softmax. This is exactly the score used for conformal multiple-choice QA on MMLU ([Kumar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18404)). The raw distribution carries prompt and label-prior bias. Contextual calibration estimates that prior from a content-free input ([Zhao et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09690)). Batch Calibration estimates it from a batch of unlabelled test inputs and beats earlier calibration methods by at least 6% across more than 10 tasks ([Zhou et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17249)). PriDe removes the option-ID prior by permuting options on a few samples ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03882)). These corrections fix *bias*, not *sharpness*, so an RLHF-tuned model is still overconfident after them. Avoid forcing reasoning tasks into constrained formats, because format restrictions degrade reasoning ([Tam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02442)). In open-weight models the accuracy cost comes mostly from the format-requesting prompt, and reasoning first and formatting in a second pass recovers most of the loss ([arXiv 2604.03616](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03616)).

**A classification head on the last-token hidden state, trained jointly with LoRA**, is the most direct fit. Hugging Face's `*ForSequenceClassification` classes with PEFT `task_type=SEQ_CLS` implement it. A 2025 study found that such heads on causal LLMs up to 8B match or beat fine-tuned BERT on single-label classification with 10–30x fewer trainable parameters ([arXiv 2512.12677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12677)). Cross-entropy is a strictly proper scoring rule, so the head is trained toward calibration by construction. The remaining miscalibration comes from overfitting, especially on small data. Laplace-LoRA fixes that post hoc and substantially lowers ECE and NLL without changing training ([Yang et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13111)). A hybrid option is to fine-tune the *label tokens* of the existing LM head, which keeps zero-shot label semantics. One caveat is that no head-to-head study reports ECE or Brier for a head versus label-token LoRA on the same task. That is an open question, and a cheap pilot experiment.

**Post-hoc temperature scaling** fits one scalar on held-out NLL, preserves argmax accuracy, and typically needs a few hundred to about a thousand labels ([Guo et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)). LLM-specific variants learn the temperature from features. Thermometer cut LLaMA-2-Chat-7B's MMLU ECE from 0.260 to 0.078 *without labels for the target task*, close to the 0.062 reached by temperature scaling with full labels ([Shen et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08819)). Adaptive Temperature Scaling improves post-RLHF calibration by 10–50% with no accuracy cost ([Xie et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19817)).

**Calibration-aware RL** (RLCR's correctness-plus-Brier reward) matters when the model must *reason and then state* a confidence. That is a System Two setting: multi-token, slow, and still at ECE of about 0.2 out of distribution ([Damani et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16806v2)). RLCR's own baselines show that a cheap BCE-trained classifier on top of the RLVR model already reaches HotPotQA ECE 0.07. For fixed-label one-pass decisions, a supervised head is the simpler and better-supported choice.

On top of any of these scores, **split conformal prediction** produces sets with a finite-sample guarantee P(y ∈ C(x)) ≥ 1−α, using only an exchangeable calibration set ([Angelopoulos & Bates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)). Set size tracks accuracy and supports a clean "act if one label, otherwise defer" policy ([Kumar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18404)). Jev's signature feature is many questions answered in parallel over one shared state. For that, prefix caching costs only the suffix tokens per extra question. Masked packing, in which each question attends only to the context and itself, reached up to 7x throughput over batching with 91–100% agreement ([IPPD, arXiv 2609.05707](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05707)). Nobody has yet evaluated *calibration* under packed multi-question scoring.

| Approach | Cost per decision | Labels needed | Calibration | Main pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-shot label logits | 1 prefill | none | poor for instruct models | prompt, label and position bias |
| + Batch Calibration / PriDe | 1 prefill | none | bias fixed, sharpness not | still overconfident |
| + temperature scaling | 1 prefill | ~100s–1k | good in-distribution | breaks under shift |
| LoRA + classification head | 1 prefill | ~1k–10k | proper-loss trained, plus temperature scaling | fixed label set; small-data overconfidence |
| RLCR verbalized confidence | full decode | RL rollouts | best when reasoning needed | slow; OOD ECE ~0.2 |
| Conformal wrapper | +0 | calibration split | marginal coverage guarantee | exchangeability; not conditional |

## Jev's calibration rests on vendor claims and Clef is the only open template

TypeSafe AI came out of stealth on 2026-09-15 with a $40M seed and Jev, billed as "the first System One model" ([flaviocopes.com](https://flaviocopes.com/jev/)). Its own docs describe the interface clearly. **Choice** picks among up to 255 options, **Score** rates on a 2–10-level rubric, and **Noul** returns P(true) for a statement. All three return probabilities and confidence, and they "can be mixed in a single API call and evaluate in parallel" against a shared state ([docs.typesafe.ai](https://docs.typesafe.ai)). The docs say almost nothing about the architecture. TechCrunch reports a transformer-based model that is "not an LLM", outputs probabilities rather than text, and was trained only on synthetic data. The CEO, Diogo Almeida, an InstructGPT co-author ([arXiv 2203.02155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)), "remains tight-lipped" on design ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/18/a-new-kind-of-ai-model-from-a-chatgpt-inventor-is-thrilling-developers/)). The press describes him as having "invented" RLHF. That overstates it: RLHF for deep RL is credited to [Christiano et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741). RLCD is defined only as "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, which optimizes for probabilities that match outcomes" ([flaviocopes.com](https://flaviocopes.com/jev/)). Turing Post states that TypeSafe "has not published enough technical detail to separate RLCD's contribution from the model architecture, data, or serving stack" ([Turing Post](https://www.turingpost.com/p/what-is-jev-rlcd)). To its credit, TypeSafe publishes a candid failure page. Jev 1.13 "does not count reliably" and struggles with dates, multi-hop indirection, irrelevant detail, injected content and contradictory instructions ([Jev 1.13 Jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13.md)). Its "zero hallucination" claim means only that outputs cannot fall outside the schema; a valid but wrong label remains possible ([innFactory](https://innfactory.ai/en/blog/jev-system-one-model-classifier-not-llm/)).

The pricing "conflict" in coverage is only a change of units: $42 per billion is the same as $0.042 per million. The latency spread likely reflects client location, since Jev is hosted on the US West Coast, and the fact that a competitor chose the test setup for the 524 ms figure. The only artefact said to track Jev's calibration is the 43-benchmark "Jev Decision Index", described as maintained by TypeSafe. Cloudflare's published table from it shows accuracy and latency but no calibration numbers ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-decision-models/)).

**Cloudflare Clef** is the open counterpart. Clef and Clef-flash are Apache-2.0 weights on Hugging Face and Workers AI, described as "fully Jev-API compatible". The published architecture is a frozen Qwen backbone (Qwen3.8-27B for Clef, Qwen3.5-9B for Clef-flash) with rank-256 LoRA adapters. It runs a prefill-only pass, then two-stage attention routing in which each valid choice extracts context, and a joint schema head emits one logit per allowed option with a per-question softmax ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-decision-models/); [HF card](https://huggingface.co/Cloudflare/clef)). Training is supervised, using label-smoothed cross-entropy plus a Brier loss on synthetic data that permutes field orders, prompts and schemas. That is the head-plus-proper-score recipe from the previous section, made explicit. Cloudflare's self-reported numbers on the Jev Decision Index put Clef at BANKING77 F1 94.2% versus Jev's 79.7%, and Clef-flash at a 38.8 ms median. The Register notes that these await independent verification, that the training data is proprietary, and that serving takes about 41 GB (Clef-flash) or 85 GB (Clef) at 64k context ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/01/cloudflare-tries-to-outplay-jev-with-open-weight-clef-models/5300649)). The one independent comparison found Clef-flash at 66.7% versus Jev's 71.4% on the same 42 decisions, agreeing on 35. It also found that GGUF quantizations drop the decision head, so running Clef-flash requires PyTorch and custom code ([dev.to](https://dev.to/prodbymarcu/i-benchmarked-cloudflares-new-open-decision-model-against-the-hosted-api-its-trying-to-replace-2ded)). The HF card's 16,384-token default input conflicts with the blog's 64k. No ECE or Brier has been published for Clef either.

**On "Qwen3.8": confidence is moderate-to-high that it exists.** The Jev/Clef research flagged "Qwen3.8-27B" as unverified. That flag rested on not finding the model while reading Cloudflare-side sources (blog, HF card, The Register), which all trace back to one vendor's naming. The model-landscape research then found a primary source: the QwenLM/Qwen3.8 GitHub repository lists **Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B (2026-08-12) and Qwen3.8-27B (2026-08-14)** as Apache-2.0 open weights with 262,144-token context ([QwenLM/Qwen3.8](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3.8)). The Qwen blog and secondary coverage of an API-only Qwen3.8-Max support it ([Qwen blog](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen3.8)). The release therefore looks real. Still unverified: Qwen3.8-27B's architecture, and whether Clef's HF `base_model` field actually points to it. There is also **no small Qwen3.6 or Qwen3.8 model**. Qwen3.6 open weights are only 27B dense and 35B-A3B MoE ([HF collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/Qwen/qwen36)), and the current small line is **Qwen3.5 0.8B/2B/4B/9B** (2026-03-02).

Whether Jev works like Clef is unknown. What RL would add over supervised proper-score training is also unexplained, since cross-entropy already rewards calibration. A reasonable reading is that RLCD is a branded proper-scoring-rule objective on synthetic labelled decisions, given Almeida's RLHF lineage and RLCR's precedent. That reading is speculation. For research purposes the conclusion is the same either way: neither system's calibration is established, and any claim must be measured on your own labelled data.

## On one A100 40GB, use bf16 LoRA on Qwen3.5-4B or -9B

Model size sets the budget. Unsloth reports bf16 LoRA memory for Qwen3.5 at **3 GB (0.8B), 5 GB (2B), 10 GB (4B), 22 GB (9B) and 56 GB (27B)**, and 74 GB for the 35B-A3B MoE. It also warns: "It is not recommended to do QLoRA (4-bit) training on the Qwen3.5 models, no matter MoE or dense" ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5/fine-tune.md)). The common plan of "QLoRA a 27B" is therefore off the table for this family. Use Qwen3.5-4B as the main base, 9B as the scale-up, and 0.8B or 2B for fast ablations. Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.8-27B work at most as FP8 or 4-bit zero-shot scorers. Clef-flash's reported 41 GB at 64k context should drop under 40 GB at the short contexts of tabular rows, which makes it a usable off-the-shelf comparator. That estimate is untested. Gemma 4 (E2B, E4B, 12B; Apache 2.0) is a sensible second family for cross-family robustness ([Gemma releases](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/releases)). Qwen3.5 checkpoints include a vision encoder, and the Qwen3.6-27B card documents a hybrid Gated-DeltaNet/attention stack ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B)). Check that the `*ForSequenceClassification` class and your LoRA target modules work for the exact checkpoint before you commit. Disable thinking (`enable_thinking=False`) so that label probabilities are not conditioned on a sampled reasoning trace.

The software floor is Python 3.10 or later. TRL 1.14.1 requires it ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/trl/)), vLLM 0.30 supports Python 3.10–3.14 and ships CUDA 12.9/13.0 wheels ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/getting_started/installation/gpu/)), and Qwen3.5 needs transformers v5 ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.5/fine-tune.md)). Build a fresh Python 3.12 environment, choose the torch CUDA build from `nvidia-smi`, and keep vLLM in a separate environment because it pins its own torch. For one-pass label scoring vLLM is optional: batched HF forward passes are enough. If you want an RLCR-style experiment later, TRL's GRPOTrainer accepts custom Python reward functions, which is the hook for a Brier reward ([TRL docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/grpo_trainer)). Unsloth GRPO on Qwen3.5 needs `fast_inference=False`.

| Stage | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Baselines | Zero-shot label logits with Batch Calibration and PriDe, on MMLU-Pro/ARC-C/BoolQ and your task | Shows how miscalibrated the instruct model is before training |
| 1. Train | LoRA (r=16, α=16–32, lr ≈1e-4–2e-4, 1–3 epochs) with a classification head *and*, separately, label-token SFT; optional Brier term as in Clef | Cross-entropy is proper; head vs label-token calibration is an open question worth answering |
| 2. Calibrate | Fit temperature (plus bias) on a held-out split; try Laplace-LoRA if data are small | Removes residual overconfidence without changing accuracy |
| 3. Wrap | Split conformal with α=0.1 (class-conditional if imbalanced) via MAPIE v1 or by hand | Coverage guarantee and a defer policy |
| 4. Report | Accuracy/macro-F1, NLL, Brier, 15-bin top-label and classwise ECE, reliability diagram, AUROC/risk–coverage, set size and coverage, exact prompt | Matches the 2026 evaluation critiques |

The temperature fit fits in a few lines and transfers directly from vision practice:

```python
T = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.ones(1, device=logits_val.device))
opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([T], lr=0.1, max_iter=200)
def closure():
    opt.zero_grad(); loss = F.cross_entropy(logits_val / T, y_val); loss.backward(); return loss
opt.step(closure)   # then use softmax(logits_test / T)
```

The hyperparameters above are community defaults; only r=16/α=16 comes from Unsloth's guide. No measured A100 throughput figures were found. For short serialized rows, activation memory is small and batch size is the main lever.

## Sperm motility tests whether the LLM earns its place

Eight CASA kinematic features (VCL, VSL, VAP, LIN, STR, WOB, ALH, BCF) form a small, purely numeric table, and on such tables the literature is unkind to LLMs. TabLLM beats XGBoost and TabPFN only in the very-few-shot regime ([Hegselmann et al., AISTATS 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/hegselmann23a/hegselmann23a.pdf)). Once labels accumulate, gradient-boosted trees overtake LLM prediction at far lower compute ([arXiv 2411.04324](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04324v1)). A 186-paper review concludes that where direct tests exist, apparent LLM parity "is traceable to information asymmetry, benchmark contamination, or computation performed outside the language model" ([Rezaee 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28980)). TabPFN-3 claims to beat 8-hour-tuned GBDTs on datasets up to 1M rows, though that is an author claim ([arXiv 2605.13986](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13986)). Classical ML already reaches up to about 89% for progressive/intermediate/hyperactivated tracks with SVMs on CASA features ([Asian J Androl](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13065317/)). These details come from abstracts, because the full texts were not accessible. Motility "fingerprints" also suggest that kinematics vary continuously rather than falling into crisp classes ([bioRxiv 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.20.689493.full.pdf)). That favours probabilistic outputs and makes calibration the right yardstick rather than accuracy alone.

The biggest threat to validity is **label leakage**. If the labels were produced by CASA threshold rules on VSL, VAP or STR, every model just relearns the rule, and near-100% accuracy means nothing. Use expert video-review labels, or frame the work explicitly as rule emulation. The exact WHO 2021 (6th edition) category definitions, and whether that edition prescribes kinematic thresholds, should be checked against the manual itself; this research could not retrieve it. The honest baseline ladder runs from the CASA/WHO rule, to multinomial logistic regression, to LightGBM or XGBoost with temperature or isotonic calibration, to TabPFN, and only then to the LLM (zero-shot, few-shot, then LoRA head or label-token). All of them should be evaluated on **donor-grouped splits** (GroupKFold by sample), so that tracks from one ejaculate never straddle train and test, using the same NLL/Brier/ECE/conformal reporting.

Serialize consistently, for example "VCL: 85.2 µm/s; VSL: 40.1 µm/s; … BCF: 11.2 Hz. Category? A) progressive B) non-progressive C) immotile". Round numbers to fixed precision, since serialization choices measurably move few-shot results ([arXiv 2312.12464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.12464)). Read P(A/B/C) from the next-token logits, and debias option order. The pre-registrable hypothesis is that the LLM *will not* beat calibrated GBDT or TabPFN on numeric features alone, but may close the gap or win in three settings. The first is few-shot adaptation to a new lab, CASA device or frame rate with only a few dozen labelled tracks. The second is fusing free-text lab notes and metadata (medium, temperature, time since collection) that tabular models cannot ingest. The third is aggregating calibrated per-track probabilities into sample-level WHO percentages with uncertainty intervals. No published study applying LLMs to sperm-motility classification was found, so a careful negative or mixed result is itself publishable. Any natural-language explanations the model produces should be checked against the tabular model's feature attributions before being trusted.

## Conclusion

The "new kind of model" framing around Jev obscures how little of the idea is new. One pass, one logit per option, a proper scoring rule and a temperature is the standard recipe for calibrated classifiers, applied to an LLM backbone over a typed schema. Clef's published design shows that open weights reach the same interface. What remains unproven, for Jev, Clef and the academic methods alike, is calibration *under shift*. RLCR's best out-of-distribution ECE is still around 0.2, and Jev's own failure list (counting, dates, multi-hop reasoning, injected content) describes exactly the inputs that move a model off its calibration set. The open research gap is therefore an empirical one. Nobody has published head-versus-label-token calibration on the same task, calibration under multi-question packing, or any reliability diagram for a commercial decision model.

For a CV-trained PhD student this is good news. The skills that matter most are ones you already have: proper losses, temperature scaling, reliability diagrams, grouped splits and honest baselines. The LLM-specific skills are narrow and learnable: reading label logits, debiasing option order, and turning off thinking. In the sperm-motility work, the useful scientific question is not whether an LLM can classify tracks. It is whether a calibrated language-model backbone adds anything a calibrated tree cannot, and under which data regime. Answering that rigorously, with a conformal defer policy, is a stronger contribution than a higher accuracy number.
